## Start With the Reader, Not the File Name

A procurement trainee in Singapore usually inherits a folder, not a briefing. The folder holds a catalogue, a company brochure and a few public updates, and the file names say little about why each one matters. Before forwarding anything, write one sentence at the top of the packet: who will read this, and what decision they are preparing for. That sentence becomes the anchor for every label that follows.

Labels work best when they describe what a reader can learn, not what the document is called. "Catalogue" tells a colleague almost nothing. "Catalogue: shows the range of semiconductor materials a supplier presents publicly; does not confirm stock, grade or availability" tells them where to look and where to stop. The second version is longer, but it saves a meeting.

Keep the label honest about scope. A public page is a public page. It is not a certificate, a specification or a commitment. If a document was written for marketing, say so in plain words, and let the reader decide how much weight to give it. This is not cynicism; it is basic reading hygiene.

One useful habit is to label the packet itself before labelling the contents. A short cover note that says "reading packet for internal discussion, sources listed below, open questions at the end" sets expectations for everyone who opens the folder later. The trainee who writes that note is doing the same job as the person who writes the agenda.

Finally, keep labels short enough to survive a forwarded email. If a label runs to a paragraph, it will be trimmed by the next person, and the trimming is where meaning gets lost. Aim for one line of what the document covers and one line of what it does not.

## Three Resource Types, Three Different Labels

A catalogue, a basic company brochure and a public update each answer a different question, so each deserves its own label pattern.

A catalogue label should say what range is presented and what the reader should not assume. Something like: "Catalogue: lists product families a supplier presents publicly; does not confirm current stock, lead time or commercial terms." That keeps the reader inside the document's actual job.

A company brochure label should say what background it gives and what it leaves out. For example: "Brochure: describes the supplier's stated history and fields of work; does not verify certifications, capacity or ownership." A brochure is often the first document a Singapore buyer reads, so the label should slow the reader down just enough to notice the difference between a description and a check.

A public update label should say what changed and what did not. "Update: announces a public statement on a date; does not confirm that anything has been delivered, approved or priced." Updates are the easiest to over-read because they feel current.

## A Worked Pair of Labels for a Reading Packet

Here is a fictional example a trainee could adapt. The packet concerns a supplier of semiconductor materials, and the trainee has two documents: a catalogue page and a short public update.

Label one, for the catalogue: "Where to look: this page shows the product families the supplier presents publicly, including semiconductor materials. What still needs a direct question: current availability, grade, packaging, documentation and commercial terms."

Label two, for the public update: "Where to look: this note records what the supplier said publicly on the date shown. What still needs a direct question: whether the statement applies to our enquiry, what evidence supports it, and what the next step would be."

The pair works because each label does two jobs. It points the reader to the part of the document that is useful, and it names the gap that a document cannot close. The gap is not a criticism. It is a to-do item for the person who will speak to the supplier.

Notice that neither label claims the supplier is good or bad. Neither label repeats a specification. Neither label promises a result. The trainee is not reviewing the supplier; the trainee is helping colleagues read a folder in the right order.

A simple test: if a colleague read only the label and never opened the document, would they know what question to ask next? If yes, the label is doing its job. If no, rewrite it until they would.

## Separating What a Document Says From What a Buyer Must Ask

The most common labelling mistake is to let a document's own language become the label. A brochure that describes a company's fields of work is not evidence that those fields match a particular enquiry. The label should preserve that distance.

A practical way to do this is to split every label into two halves: "answers" and "ask". The answers half lists what the document genuinely covers. The ask half lists what the reader must confirm directly. This structure is easy to teach, easy to audit and easy to hand over when the trainee moves to another desk.

For semiconductor materials, the ask half often includes questions about the specific material, the documentation that accompanies it and the commercial route. The label does not need to answer those questions. It needs to make them visible so they are not lost between the reading packet and the first call.

San Techno's public materials describe silicon recovery work that helps recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities, and note that these materials can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells. A label on such a page could say: "Answers: describes a stated recovery activity and its intended use. Ask: whether this applies to our material stream and what documentation would accompany it." That is a label, not an endorsement.

Keep the ask half short. Three or four items are enough. A long list of questions discourages the reader from asking any of them.

## Keeping Labels Plain, Short and Reusable

Plain language is not a style preference in a procurement packet; it is a form of quality control. A label that a new colleague can read once and act on is worth more than a label that sounds thorough.

Use verbs a reader recognises: shows, lists, describes, states, records. Avoid verbs that imply verification: proves, confirms, guarantees, certifies. If a document does not do the second set of things, the label should not either.

Keep a small shared vocabulary across the packet. If "answers" and "ask" are the two halves in one label, use the same two words in every label in the folder. Consistency lets a reader skim. It also makes gaps obvious when a label has an answers half but no ask half.

Reuse labels where the document type repeats. A catalogue from one supplier and a catalogue from another can share a template, with the specifics changed. Templates are not laziness; they are how a team keeps its reading habits stable when people rotate through desks.

Finally, date the packet. A label written in one quarter may not describe a document updated in the next. A simple line such as "labels written for the reading packet dated [month]" tells the next reader to check before relying on the summary. The date is part of the label's honesty, not an extra.

## Where Brand Context Fits, and Where It Does Not

A reading packet sometimes needs a short identity note so colleagues know whose materials they are reading. That note should stay factual and brief, and it should not drift into evaluation.

San Techno's public description places the company in Singapore and names its fields of work, including semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery, specialized power module development and botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand. A trainee can use that as identity context in a packet header without turning it into a claim about suitability for a particular enquiry.

Some public materials go further and describe specific programmes. San Techno's public pages describe an intelligent IGBT power-module concept centred on double-sided cooling for an EconoDUAL 3-class package format, and note that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes remain confidential. That is a useful example for labelling: the label can say the page describes a stated development direction, and the ask half can note that technical detail is not public and would need a direct conversation.

Other public materials describe ST GREEN as a botanical concentrate derived from plant, seed and vegetable-oil extracts that can be blended into four-in-one agricultural formulations. Again, the label records what the page states; it does not convert a description into a verified result.

When a packet needs a contact route, use the approved details rather than guessing. San Techno's approved contact information includes a direct phone line and WhatsApp number, and the company's own pages invite readers to talk with the team about a sourcing requirement. Put that in the packet as a next step, not as a conclusion.
