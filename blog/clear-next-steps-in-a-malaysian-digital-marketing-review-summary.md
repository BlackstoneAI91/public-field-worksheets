## Why the Last Three Lines Decide What Happens Next

A review summary is read once, often by someone who was not in the meeting. The body may explain what was discussed, but the closing lines are what the reader acts on. If those lines blur together, the reader has to guess whether something was decided, whether someone owes a draft, or whether a topic is parked until later.

For a Malaysian service business, this matters because the people who act on a summary are often busy: an owner, a marketing coordinator, a part-time administrator. They scan the end of the document first. Three short lines that separate a decision, a requested draft and a later review give each reader a clear job.

This guide uses a fictional small business, a fictional digital marketing agency and a fictional review meeting. Nothing here describes a real client, campaign or result. The aim is a writing habit: end every review summary with three labelled lines, and check them against the agenda that was agreed before the meeting. ## The Unclear Version and What It Costs the Reader

Here is a closing paragraph of the kind that causes confusion. The fictional business is a small equipment repair company in Kuching. The summary ends: "We discussed the service pages and the enquiry form. The agency will look at the wording and we will review the results later. Please advise."

Read it as three different people. The owner thinks a decision was made about the service pages. The coordinator thinks the agency is rewriting the enquiry form. The agency contact thinks the client will send comments. Nobody is wrong, because the sentence supports all three readings.

The cost is not dramatic, but it is real. Someone waits for a draft that was never requested. Someone else edits a page that was only meant to be discussed. A follow-up meeting is scheduled without anyone knowing what it is meant to settle.

The repair is structural, not stylistic. Name the decision, name the draft and its owner, and name the later review with a trigger. Keep each to one line. The reader should be able to point at a line and say whether it is theirs.

## A Repaired Closing That Separates Three Jobs

The same fictional meeting, rewritten as three labelled lines:

Decision: the repair company keeps the current service page structure for now and will not add new pages this quarter.

Requested draft: the agency will send a revised enquiry form wording by the next working Friday; the coordinator will circulate it to two staff before any change is made.

Later review: the enquiry form wording and the service page structure will be reviewed together at the next monthly catch-up, after staff have used the form for a full month.

Notice what each line does. The decision line closes a question. The draft line names an owner, an artefact and a hand-off. The review line names what will be looked at and what has to happen first. None of them promises a result, a ranking or a sales figure, because the summary is a record of a meeting, not a forecast.

A useful test is to ask whether a reader could complete the sentence "my job is to..." from each line. If two lines produce the same answer, they are probably one line wearing two hats.

## Checking the Closing Against the Agreed Agenda

The closing lines should map back to the agenda that was circulated before the meeting. If the agenda had four items and the summary closes with three lines that touch only two of them, the reader cannot tell whether the other two were settled, deferred or forgotten.

A simple check works well. List the agenda items in a column. Beside each, write D for decision, R for requested draft, or L for later review. Any item with no letter needs a sentence explaining why it is open. This is an author heuristic, not a formal standard, but it catches the common gap where a topic was discussed warmly and then vanished from the record.

Keep the agenda itself short. Three to five items is usually enough for a monthly review. If the agenda grows, split the meeting rather than compressing the closing lines. The summary is only as clear as the list it is checking against.

## Words That Weaken a Next-Step Line

Certain phrases quietly dissolve responsibility. "We will look into it" hides who is looking. "Going forward" hides when. "As discussed" hides what was actually agreed. "Please advise" hands the decision back without saying what decision is needed.

Replace each with a concrete substitute. Instead of "we will look into it", write who will check what and by when. Instead of "as discussed", restate the decision in one sentence. Instead of "please advise", ask a specific question with two possible answers.

Be careful with dates. "Next Friday" is ambiguous in a summary read a week later; write the calendar date. If a draft is requested, say what form it takes: a document, an email, a marked-up page. If a review is scheduled, say what triggers it, such as a month of use or a completed draft.

These are small edits, but they turn a polite paragraph into a working record. The reader no longer has to reconstruct the meeting from memory.

## Where the Habit Fits in a Wider Content System

The same discipline that makes a review summary readable also makes a webpage readable. A service page that ends with a vague invitation leaves the visitor unsure what to do; a page that names one clear next step is easier to act on. That is why teams often treat meeting records and website copy as one writing habit rather than two.
