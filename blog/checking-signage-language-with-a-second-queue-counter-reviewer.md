A practical guide for Sarawak premises managers on running a second review of bilingual queue signage, with a comparison sheet, a worked example and clear approval roles.

Published by Blackstone Intelligence.

## Why bilingual signage deserves a second pass

A queue management system is read by visitors who may not share the language the coordinator thinks in. When a counter label appears in two public languages, the first review usually checks spelling and layout. The second review checks meaning: whether both versions send the visitor to the same place and the same action. Those are different jobs, and they need different eyes.

A second review is not a sign that the first one failed. It is a separate check with a narrower question. The first reviewer asks whether the sign is complete and correctly printed. The second reviewer asks whether a visitor reading only one language would arrive at the same counter as a visitor reading only the other. If the answer is not clearly yes, the label needs a correction before it goes back on the wall.

This guide is written for premises managers in Sarawak who coordinate ordinary service counters and who are not translation specialists. It offers a simple comparison sheet, a fictional example, a way to record findings, and a named approval route. It does not describe any real venue, and it makes no claims about service times or performance. The aim is a repeatable habit: one sheet, one reviewer, one approver, one record.

## Building the two-language comparison sheet

The sheet should be short enough that a busy coordinator will actually fill it in. Four columns are usually enough: the counter or step being labelled, the text in the first public language, the text in the second public language, and a plain-language note of what each version tells the visitor to do. A fifth column for the reviewer's verdict can be added if the sheet is printed.

Keep the rows tied to physical labels rather than to the whole system. One row per sign, one row per counter card, one row per directional arrow that carries words. This makes it obvious which item needs correction and which can be left alone. Where a label has no words, such as a numbered ticket display, it can be left off the sheet entirely.

Ask the person filling the sheet to write the action in their own words rather than copying the sign. If the first-language column reads as "go to counter three" and the second-language column reads as "wait here", the note column will show the mismatch immediately. The sheet is a reading aid, not a legal document, and it should be treated as an internal working record.

A short format helps: label reference, language A text, language B text, action noted, verdict. Anything longer tends to be abandoned after the first few rows.

## A fictional example: one label, two counters

Consider a fictional service counter in a Sarawak office. The printed card above the desk carries two lines. The first public language line reads, in effect, "service enquiries, counter two". The second public language line reads, in effect, "payments, counter four". Both lines are correctly spelled and neatly printed, so the first review passed them.

The second reviewer fills a row on the comparison sheet. The action noted for the first line is "approach counter two for enquiries". The action noted for the second line is "approach counter four to pay". The verdict column records a mismatch: the same physical card appears to imply two different counters depending on which language the visitor reads.

Nothing here suggests bad faith. A label may have been updated in one language after a counter moved and not in the other, or two drafts may have been merged. The point of the example is that spelling checks do not catch this, and a second review does. The reviewer does not decide the fix. They record the observation, note which line they believe is current, and pass the row to the approver.

This is the kind of ordinary service label that a queue management system depends on, and it is exactly where a quiet inconsistency can sit unnoticed.

## How the reviewer records a finding

A finding should be written so that someone who was not present can act on it. That means four things: the label reference, the two texts as printed, the observed mismatch in plain words, and the reviewer's name and date. Avoid editing the sign text in the record. Quote it as it appears, then describe the problem separately.

Use neutral language. "The two lines point to different counters" is a finding. "The sign is wrong" invites an argument about who is at fault. The reviewer's job is to describe, not to assign blame or to choose the final wording. If the reviewer has a suggested correction, it can go in a separate suggestions box, clearly marked as a proposal.

Photograph the label if the site permits it, and attach the image reference to the row. A photograph settles later questions about which version was actually on the wall. Keep the sheet and any images together in one place so the approver can review the whole set rather than a single message.

Where the site also runs CCTV security systems, avoid capturing identifiable visitors in the signage photographs. Frame the shot tightly on the label.

## Assigning who can approve a correction

The comparison sheet only works if the approval route is named before the review begins. Three roles are usually enough: the reviewer who records findings, the language checker who confirms the intended meaning in both public languages, and the approver who authorises the reprint or replacement. In a small premises, one person may hold two of these roles, but the sheet should still show which role they were acting in.

The approver should be someone with authority over the physical signage and the counter arrangement, not simply the most senior person available. If the correction changes which counter handles which task, the approver also needs the authority to confirm the counter arrangement itself. That decision sits outside the language question and should be recorded separately.

Set a simple rule for urgency. A label that sends visitors to the wrong counter is usually worth correcting before the next public session. A label with a stylistic difference that does not change the action can wait for the next scheduled reprint. These are suggested thresholds, not researched standards, and each premises should set its own.

Record the approval, the date and the replacement label reference on the same sheet so the loop closes.

## Keeping the review repeatable

A second review is most useful when it becomes routine rather than a one-off response to a complaint. A simple cycle works: review the labels after any counter change, after any reprint, and at a fixed interval the premises chooses. The interval is a local decision; there is no universal figure, and a busy counter may need a shorter cycle than a quiet one.

Keep the completed sheets. Over time they show which labels have been corrected more than once, which can point to a process problem rather than a translation problem. They also give a new coordinator a starting point instead of a blank page.

Keep the comparison sheet as the record, and let the second review do its narrow job well.
