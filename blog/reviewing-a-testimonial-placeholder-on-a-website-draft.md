## Why a Placeholder Needs a Label Before Publication

A draft service page often carries a line such as "Insert client quote here" or a grey box where a testimonial should sit. These lines are useful during drafting because they reserve space and remind the writer that social proof belongs on the page. The risk begins when the draft moves toward publication and nobody checks whether the placeholder was replaced, removed or deliberately kept.

A simple worksheet solves this. Give every testimonial slot a status: placeholder, quote awaiting clearance, or approved text. A placeholder is a note to the team, not content for the public. A quote awaiting clearance is real material that cannot appear until the named customer has agreed to its use. Approved text is the only category that may go live.

The worksheet should also record who owns each line and the date it was last checked. That single column prevents the common situation where three people each assume someone else confirmed the quote.

For a Malaysian service business, the worksheet is worth keeping alongside the draft rather than in a separate folder. When the page is reviewed, the reviewer sees the status of every testimonial line at a glance. If the business works with a web design services partner or an in-house team, the same worksheet travels with the draft and removes guesswork about what is finished.

None of this requires legal training. It is an editorial habit: label the line, name the owner, and do not publish an unlabelled slot.

## Separating Three Kinds of Line in the Draft

The worksheet works best when the reviewer sorts every testimonial-related line into one of three buckets.

The first is the placeholder: bracketed notes, lorem-style filler, sample names, or a stock quote that was pasted in as a shape-holder. These should never survive to publication. The second is the real customer quote still awaiting clearance. It may be accurate, but it is not yet usable. The third is an approved factual description of the service, which is not a testimonial at all but a plain statement of what the business does.

That third bucket matters because it is often the safest replacement. Instead of a quote, the page can describe the service in factual terms drawn from the business's own records. For example, a page might state which services are offered and how enquiries are handled, without claiming results or satisfaction.

## Marking a Line for Replacement: A Reviewer's Method

A reviewer needs a repeatable way to mark a line so the writer knows exactly what to do. The method below is an author heuristic, not a standard, and teams can adjust it.

First, highlight the line and write its status in the margin: P for placeholder, C for awaiting clearance, A for approved. Second, write the intended replacement in one sentence. Third, note the source of the replacement: the business's own service list, an approved customer statement, or nothing yet. Fourth, add the reviewer's initials and the date.

A line marked P with no replacement source should usually be deleted rather than rewritten, because inventing a quote is never acceptable. A line marked C stays in the draft but is visibly blocked from publication until clearance arrives. A line marked A can proceed.

The reviewer should also check that the surrounding paragraph still reads well once the line is removed. Placeholders often sit inside sentences that depend on them, such as "As one client put it, ...". Removing the quote may leave a fragment, so the reviewer marks the whole sentence, not just the quote.

Finally, the reviewer records the decision in the worksheet so the next person does not reopen a settled question.

## Three Invented Before-and-After Examples

The examples below are invented for illustration. They do not describe any real customer, review or result.

Example one. Before: "[Client name] says our team is the best in Kuching." After: the line is deleted and replaced with a factual sentence describing the service offered. The reviewer marks it P and notes that no approved quote exists.

Example two. Before: "Our client told us the new site doubled their enquiries." After: the line is moved to a holding section marked C, with a note that the customer must confirm the wording before it appears. The page publishes without it until clearance arrives.

Example three. Before: a grey box labelled "testimonial" with no text. After: the box is removed and the section is rewritten around a plain description of how enquiries are handled. The reviewer marks it P and records that the section no longer depends on social proof.

In each case the reviewer's job is the same: identify the line, decide its status, and write the replacement in plain language. The worksheet keeps the three examples consistent, and a writer returning to the draft later can see why each change was made.

## Keeping Service Descriptions Factual and Checkable

When a testimonial is removed, the page can feel thin. The fix is not to invent a replacement quote but to describe the service more precisely, using facts the business can stand behind.

A factual description answers simple questions: what is offered, who it is for, and how an enquiry is handled. It avoids superlatives and avoids claims about outcomes. If the business has a published service list, that list is the safest source.

A reviewer should also check that any factual line is still current. Service lists change, and a description copied from an old draft may no longer match what the business offers. The worksheet can carry a "last verified" date for each factual line, so the page is checked before each major update rather than assumed correct.

Where a claim cannot be sourced, it stays out. Missing evidence is better than a confident sentence nobody can support.

## A Short Prepublication Checklist

Before the page goes live, the reviewer can run a short checklist. The timings and scores below are examples, not research findings.

One: every testimonial slot has a status of P, C or A. Two: no line marked P remains in the publishable draft. Three: every line marked C is visibly blocked and has a named owner. Four: every factual service line can be traced to an internal source. Five: the page reads naturally with the placeholders removed. Six: the worksheet is stored with the draft and dated.

A reviewer might allow ten to fifteen minutes for this pass on a single service page, though the real time depends on how many testimonial lines the draft contains. Teams that publish often may prefer a shorter check focused only on status and ownership.

The checklist is not a legal review and does not replace one. It is an editorial control that keeps unapproved material off the page. Where a business wants a formal review of customer consent or contract terms, that is a separate step with its own adviser.

Used consistently, the worksheet turns a vague worry about testimonials into a set of visible decisions, and the published page carries only text the business can stand behind.
