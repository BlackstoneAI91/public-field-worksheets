## Why a Shared Screen Needs a Spoken Method

Two people can sit at the same laptop and still compare different things. One reads the photo, the other reads the title, and both leave with a different idea of what the listing says. When only one person can scroll, the second shopper becomes a listener, and listening is easy to get wrong.

The fix is not more talking. It is a fixed order of talking. Before you open any dog accessories online store, agree that the person holding the mouse reads aloud in three parts: the label, the option shown, and whether the second shopper confirms the match. Nothing is assumed from a glance at the picture.

This matters because a listing is a bundle of separate pieces of text. A title, a variant name, a photo caption and a seller note can each describe the item slightly differently. The listener cannot see which one the controller is looking at unless the controller says so.

## Set Roles Before the First Scroll

Decide who drives and who checks. The driver controls the screen, opens tabs and reads text aloud. The checker keeps a written note of what was confirmed and what is still unclear. Swap roles only at a clear stopping point, never mid-listing, because a half-swapped session is where labels get mixed up.

Agree on a small vocabulary first. Use the word label for the exact text on the page, option for the specific variant or choice being shown, and match only when both people agree the label and the option describe the same thing. Avoid vague words like that one or the usual size, which mean different things to different people.

Keep one shared note open. Each line should record the label as written, the option selected, and a short confirmed or unclear marker. The checker owns this note, not the driver, so the person reading aloud is not also the person deciding what was heard.

## The Three-Line Read-Back Example

Here is a fictional example of the format, using invented shoppers and invented listing text. It is a demonstration of structure, not a description of any real seller.

Line one, the label: "The title says multi-pocket walking pouch, and the caption says four openings." The driver reads the exact words and does not paraphrase.

Line two, the option: "The selected option says large, and the photo shows a different colour than the option name." The driver names what is selected and flags any mismatch between the option and the image.

Line three, the confirmed match: "Confirmed: we are both looking at the large option, and we both read four openings in the caption. Unclear: whether the photo matches the large option." The checker repeats back what was agreed and what was not.

That third line is the important one. It separates what is settled from what is still open, so the next scroll does not reopen a question you already closed. If the checker cannot repeat the label back, the driver reads it again rather than moving on.

## Handling Labels That Do Not Match the Photo

Mismatches are common enough that you should expect them rather than treat them as a red flag on their own. A listing may show a photo from an earlier batch, a caption written for a different variant, or an option name that does not appear anywhere in the image.

When this happens, do not guess which one is correct. Write the mismatch down in the shared note exactly as you see it, then move to the next listing. Come back only if the item still matters after you have compared the others.

A useful habit is to read the label twice: once as the driver sees it, and once as the checker repeats it. If the two readings differ, the label is ambiguous for your purposes, whatever the seller intended. That is a reading problem, not a claim about the seller.

Keep the note factual. Write what the page says, not what you think it means. Phrases like probably the same thing or must be the newer version belong in a separate questions column, clearly marked as your own interpretation.

## A Short Message to a Vendor About an Ambiguous Label

Sometimes the cleanest next step is to ask. Keep the message short, specific and free of assumptions. A fictional example you can adapt:

"Hello, I am comparing your listing for the multi-pocket walking pouch. The title says four openings but the photo appears to show a different layout, and the selected option is large. Could you confirm which description applies to the large option? Thank you."

Notice what the message does not do. It does not ask for a recommendation, does not mention price, and does not suggest the listing is wrong. It names the label, the option and the specific mismatch, which makes it easy for the seller to answer.

Send one message per question. If you have three unclear listings, send three short messages rather than one long one, so each reply can be matched to its listing in your shared note. Record the date you asked and the reply you received alongside the original line.

## Closing the Session and Keeping the Note Useful

End with a short review of the shared note. Read each line aloud one last time and mark it settled or still open. Anything still open should carry a next action, such as ask the vendor or check again later, so the note does not become a list of loose ends.

Store the note where both shoppers can reach it. A single shared document is easier to keep consistent than two separate copies that drift apart. If you start a new session, begin by reading the open lines from the previous one rather than starting fresh.

Finally, keep the method in proportion. It is a way to slow down a comparison so two people see the same text, not a scoring system and not a guarantee that a listing is accurate. Treat any timings or checklists you build around it as your own household convention.
