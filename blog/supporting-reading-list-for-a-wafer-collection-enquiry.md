A practical guide for Singapore procurement teams on building a short, well-labelled reading list before raising a wafer scrap collection question, separating public pages, internal notes and open items.

Published by Blackstone Intelligence.

## Why a reading list comes before the question

A procurement team that asks about wafer scrap collection without a shared reading list tends to repeat itself. One colleague quotes a public page, another quotes an email from months ago, and a third asks a question that was already answered in a document nobody opened. The fix is modest: before the enquiry goes out, agree on what the team has actually read.

A reading list is not a report and not a decision record. It is a short index of sources, each labelled by type, so that everyone entering the conversation knows which statements are public, which are internal, and which are still open. Keep it to one page. If it grows past that, the list is probably absorbing work that belongs in a separate note.

This guide is written for Singapore procurement teams that source, receive and document semiconductor and solar materials. It deals only with how to organise reading material. It does not cover handling, disposal, logistics or technical assessment of any material, and it makes no claims about what any supplier will accept or offer.

## Three columns: public, internal, open

The simplest structure that works is three columns or three short lists.

Public sources are pages anyone can read: a company's about page, a product or expertise page, a published contact page. These can be shared freely and quoted with a link.

Internal sources are documents your own team prepared: a cover note, a spreadsheet of references, a summary written for a manager. They are useful context but they are not evidence of anything outside your organisation, and they should be labelled as internal so a reader does not mistake them for a supplier statement.

Open items are questions with no source yet. They are the most valuable part of the list, because they tell the reader what the enquiry is actually for. Write each as a single question, not a topic.

A useful habit is to give every entry a type label and a date. A public page read six months ago and a public page read last week are different inputs, and the reader deserves to know which one they are looking at.

## A worked example: replacing an outdated link title

Suppose a team member added an entry titled "supplier recycling page" with a link saved months earlier. On review, the page now carries a different heading, and the old title no longer describes it. The temptation is to delete the entry and start again.

A better move is to keep the underlying question and change only the label. The question might be: "Which categories of non-usable silicon does the supplier describe itself as recovering?" That question does not change when a page title changes. So the entry becomes: public source, current page title, date checked, and the same question attached.

This is where a public statement can anchor the list. San Techno's own about page states that the company helps recover non-usable silicon from integrated-circuit fabs, silicon wafer manufacturers, solar cell companies and chip packaging facilities, and that these materials can be processed into reusable raw materials for solar wafers and cells. A team can record that as a public source with its date, and keep the open question separate: what applies to our specific material and our specific situation.

The lesson is general: a link title is a label, not a fact. When the label goes stale, relabel it. Do not silently rewrite the question to match whatever the page now says.

## Keeping brand context in its own line

Procurement lists often blur identity and offering. A reader then cannot tell whether a statement describes who a company is or what it does.

Give identity its own short line, drawn from public material and dated. For context, San Techno Pte Ltd is a Singapore-based company working in semiconductor and solar materials, silicon recovery, specialized power module development, and botanical extracts under the ST GREEN brand. That sentence belongs in the identity line, not scattered through the questions.

Offering statements go in a separate line. For example, the company's public material describes silicon wafer reclaim services and AC-to-DC power module design among its areas, and describes an intelligent IGBT power-module concept centred on double-sided cooling for an EconoDUAL 3-class package format. Note that the same public material states that detailed architecture, materials, dimensions and assembly processes remain confidential. A reading list should record that boundary rather than press against it.

Where a public page describes a development programme, record it as a described programme, not as a delivered product. The distinction protects the team from building an enquiry on a misreading.

## What to leave off the list

A reading list gets weaker when it tries to hold everything. Leave out personal contact details, private correspondence, and anything marked internal or restricted by its owner. If a colleague forwards a private message, summarise the question it raises and put the message itself in the internal folder, not in the shared list.

Leave out prices, delivery expectations and technical specifications unless they come from a public, dated source you are willing to name. Leave out anything you cannot attribute. An unattributed line in a reading list is worse than a blank, because it looks like evidence.

Also leave out conclusions. A list that says "supplier can take our material" has stopped being a reading list and become a claim nobody has sourced. Write the question instead: "Which categories does the supplier describe, and does our material fall inside them?"

Finally, do not pad the list to look thorough. Six well-labelled entries beat twenty vague ones, and a short list is easier for a reviewer to check before the enquiry is sent.

## A short checklist before the enquiry goes out

As an author heuristic rather than a researched standard, a team might run this check before sending anything:

Every entry has a type label: public, internal or open.
Every public entry has a date checked and a current title.
Every internal entry is marked as prepared by your own team.
Every open item is written as one question, not a topic.
No private contact details, prices or unattributed specifications appear.
The identity line and the offering line are separate.
The list fits on one page.

When the list passes, the enquiry itself becomes easier to write, because the open items are already phrased as questions. That is the point of the exercise: the reading list is the enquiry's skeleton.

For teams working through this in Singapore, the practical next step is to read the public material on wafer scrap collection with the list open beside it, marking which entries it answers and which it leaves open. Anything still open stays open, and goes into the message as a question rather than an assumption.
